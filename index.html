Juliane M. Liberto, Ph.D.

Cancer Biologist

I am a cancer biologist and translational researcher investigating how tumor biology, metabolism, and the immune microenvironment interact to drive cancer progression and therapeutic response. My research integrates molecular biology, immunology, multi-omics, bioinformatics, and preclinical models to uncover mechanisms of disease and identify opportunities for therapeutic intervention.

With experience across academic research, clinical research, and biotechnology, I bring a multidisciplinary and translational perspective to cancer research. I am passionate about conducting rigorous, collaborative research that advances our understanding of cancer and contributes to the development of more effective therapeutic strategies.

My Thesis Research

Statins, Cancer, and the Immune System

Understanding how statins reshape anti-tumor immunity

High-grade serous ovarian cancer (HGSOC) remains largely resistant to immune checkpoint blockade despite advances in cancer immunotherapy. My research investigates whether statins can reprogram the tumor immune microenvironment to enhance anti-tumor immunity. Using murine tumor models, flow cytometry, single-cell RNA sequencing, immunohistochemistry, immunofluorescence, cell culture, and complementary molecular approaches, I investigated how statin treatment influences immune cell composition and function within the tumor microenvironment. My studies demonstrate that statin treatment enhances T-cell activity within HGSOC tumors via tumor associated macrophage (TAM) reprogramming. These results support the possibility that statins may sensitize ovarian tumors to immune checkpoint blockade and could improve patient responses to these treatemnts.

Why Genetics Matter

Understanding Host Genetics in Drug–Microbiome Interactions

While statins are known to influence metabolism, relatively little is understood about their effects on host-microbe interactions. My research examines how genetic background shapes the microbiome's response to statin treatment. Using multiple inbred mouse strains, I characterize changes in microbial communities and microbial metabolites following statin treatment. My work demonstrates that host genetics strongly influence microbial responses to statins. Among the strains examined, C57BL/6 mice exhibited the most pronounced microbial changes, including an enrichment of butyrate-producing bacterial populations. Understanding how host genetics shapes microbiome responses to statins may help explain variability in drug response and uncover new links between metabolism, immunity, and microbial ecology.
